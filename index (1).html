Case studies

Revenue enablement and learning experience

These case studies reflect a consistent operating philosophy: enablement only matters when it changes what sellers do in front of customers. Rather than treating enablement as content production or event management, each initiative treats capability as an end-to-end performance system combining environment, tooling, practice, and frontline leadership coaching.

Each one includes an honest retrospective. It shows not just what went right, but what failed, what was retired, and how the system was adjusted.

Case 1: Enablement Strategy

Hundreds of sellers across multiple regions, thousands of calls analysed

Problem

Selling varied widely by region: inconsistent qualification, shallow discovery, product-led pitches. Enablement was judged on attendance, completion and satisfaction and not on impact on revenue.

Diagnosis

Sellers had the information but when frontline leaders did not coach a method, the training was treated as an interruption. Capability had to be designed as a system, with leaders owning the coaching and proficiency shown before sellers worked unassisted.

Approach

I designed a strategy that starts from the desired performance and works backwards to the actions that produce it:

  • Short self-paced pre-work replaced multi-hour lectures.
  • Mandatory roleplay on real objections replaced theoretical discussion.
  • Live territory activity was gated behind leader-graded assessments.
  • Call scorecards gave leaders a structured way to review conversations, and call, learning and content data fed their regular cadences.

Outcome

Leaders moved from observers to coaching owners, with call reviews embedded in weekly cadences. Higher-scoring sellers showed stronger pipeline progression, deal velocity and deal size. This is an association, not proof of effect, and is reported as one. The result is a repeatable method for testing whether enablement shows up in field behaviour and in deals.

Retrospective

The first scorecards had more than ten behaviours, and leaders scored inconsistently. I now cap them at three to five observable, non-negotiable behaviours. Whether the consistency improved or not is still untested.

Only a small share of calls was scored because my automated scoring criteria were restrictive, which limited generalisation. Where possible, I now design sampling upfront, agree the method with analytics before analysis starts, and compare sellers against their own earlier calls.

Case 2: Storyboard-to-SCORM Engine

Course production from six weeks to under four days per market

Problem

Each market needed localised enablement, but manual authoring took six to eight weeks per course. Regions defaulted to generic training.

Diagnosis

The bottleneck was not instructional design. It was manual asset handling and slow review cycles. Off-the-shelf generative tools invented product interfaces, which made them unsafe for training.

Approach

I built an AI-assisted pipeline that separates structure from evidence:

  • AI drafted region-specific storyboards from approved product and value-proposition sources, then generated the module code and packaging.
  • Subject experts supplied real, human-captured screen recordings for every product workflow.
  • A shared wireframe and review standard let any designer on a global team produce consistent modules.

Outcome

Production time fell from six weeks to under four days per market. This includes recording, review, and sign-off. Learning paths for three regions were delivered in parallel. This measures production speed, not learning impact.

Retrospective

Early AI-generated interface mockups were inaccurate, so I now use only captured recordings for product workflows. Regional variants without owners became a patching burden when features changed, so I now agree content ownership and update commitments with local teams before any multi-market build.

Case 3: Regionalised Onboarding

First pilot: three milestones, gated by demonstrated capability

Problem

Onboarding ran on a 30-60-90 day calendar, was centrally built and generic across regions, and left leader and buddy responsibilities vague. Progress meant days elapsed, not readiness for a customer conversation.

Diagnosis

I reframed the question from “what do new hires need to know?” to “what has to be true for them to hold a revenue-driving conversation?” The answer was a set of interdependent conditions: environment, tooling, access, relationships, a named human in the loop, and content. Content was the smallest part.

Approach

  • Three milestones, no fixed duration. Role and tooling, then product demonstration and objection handling, then territory planning and live conversations. Hires progressed on demonstrated capability.
  • Regional narratives. Region-specific content built on the engine from Case 2, so each market got local relevance without a six-week build.
  • Shared accountability matrix. Deliverables mapped across new hire, leader, buddy and enablement, so support was explicit.
  • AI support experiment. Integrated an AI agent into the workflow to answer policy and process questions from verified documentation.

Outcome

Early signal from a small pilot, compared with historical cohorts: faster role readiness and customer empathy. We saw a 30% improvement in ramp for a pilot size of three. Leader coordination fell to under 60 minutes a week. The sample size is too small to consider this a proof, but it is a very positive signal.

Retrospective

Self-paced reading could not teach complex CRM tooling. Tool competence needs live, hands-on walkthroughs and IT provisioning before Day 1, which I now treat as a precondition, not a fix.

The custom AI agents failed to provide lasting value. By Day 2, new hires bypassed the specialised agent and used native enterprise search directly, which was faster and less constrained.

Case 4: Consolidating Sales Learning and Content

One platform for learning and content, and one place for everything a buyer receives

Problem

Learning and content sat across multiple systems with duplicated resources. Sellers could not tell which version was current, and the team could not keep any of it up to date. In long sales cycles, material sent to buyers was scattered across email threads.

Diagnosis

The issue was trust in what sellers found, not the volume of it. Search across duplicates returns the wrong version, so consolidation and ownership had to come before findability.

Approach

  • Migration. I led the content team through moving a legacy learning system into one platform for learning and content, connected to the CRM, with priority assets, reusable content and templates rebuilt.
  • Audit. A cross-functional audit retired more than 500 duplicate assets, leaving one owned version of each.
  • Trackable sharing. Sellers shared content through a single digital room per deal instead of loose attachments, so everything a buyer received over a long cycle stayed in one place, and sellers could see what buyers engaged with.

Outcome

A maintainable library with clear ownership, and external sharing moved from scattered email to a systematic, trackable route.

Retrospective

I tracked user counts at launch, which show access, not active use, so I cannot show effect on deals.
